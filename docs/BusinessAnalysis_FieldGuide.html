Business Analyst Field Guide

A working knowledge base for the AI Solution Architect track. You will not become a Business Analyst here — you will learn the roughly 20% of the discipline that lets you direct an AI system generating requirements, evaluate what it produces, and approve (or reject) it with confidence.

Everything in this guide is built around one worked example so the concepts never float in the abstract: ClaimSight AI, an AI-assisted claims intake and triage initiative for a fictional mid-size insurer, Meridian Mutual Insurance. Every document — the BRD, the user stories, the traceability matrix — is written the way a real delivery team would write it, at the level of specificity an architect should expect and demand.

How to use this guide Read The Discipline section once, straight through — it is short by design. Then treat the nine documents under Deliverables as reference material: jump to whichever one you need using the left navigation, the search box in the header, or the Ask the Guide assistant. Every document can be downloaded as a standalone styled HTML file, saved as a PDF, or (where it is tabular) exported to CSV, using the toolbar at the top of that document.

What "the 20%" means here

An AI Solution Architect does not write BRDs for a living. But when an AI system drafts one in ninety seconds, someone with authority has to know whether it is any good — whether the requirements are testable, whether priorities are honest, whether a fraud-detection feature quietly has no acceptance criteria, whether a persona is decoration or a real constraint on design. That judgment is teachable in an afternoon if you focus on the right handful of ideas. This guide is that afternoon.

The Discipline

Why Business Analysis Exists

Business analysis is the translation layer between "what the business needs" and "what gets built." Skip it, and AI does not remove the risk — it just moves the risk downstream and hides it behind confident-sounding prose.

Every failed software initiative has the same autopsy: the thing that got built was not the thing that was needed. Sometimes because nobody asked the right people. Sometimes because the ask was vague ("make claims processing better") and everyone quietly filled in their own definition of "better." Sometimes because the requirements were right on day one and nobody tracked what happened when three of them changed in week six. Business analysis exists to make these failures visible before money and time are spent, not after.

An AI system can now draft a requirements document, a set of user stories, and a traceability matrix in the time it takes to read this sentence. That is a genuine capability shift. It is also exactly why the discipline matters more, not less: the AI will produce something plausible whether or not it is correct, and plausible-but-wrong is far more dangerous than obviously-incomplete, because plausible-but-wrong sails through review.

The architect's job, precisely stated You are not the one writing the requirements. You are the one who is accountable when they are wrong. That means your value is 100% in the review: catching the missing non-functional requirement, the untestable acceptance criterion, the persona that is decoration rather than a real design constraint, the requirement with no owner and no way to verify it shipped.

The Core 20%: What You Must Know

Six ideas. Master these and you can hold your own in any requirements review, AI-generated or not.

Functional vs. Non-Functional User Stories & Acceptance Criteria MoSCoW Prioritization Personas Use Cases Traceability

1. Functional vs. Non-Functional Requirements

A functional requirement (FR) describes something the system does — a behavior, a capability, an action a user or another system can trigger. "The system shall auto-route a claim to the Fast-track adjuster queue when triage confidence exceeds 85%" is functional. A non-functional requirement (NFR) describes a quality or constraint on how the system does everything it does — performance, security, availability, compliance, accessibility, scalability. "Triage decisions must return within 8 seconds for 95% of submissions" is non-functional.

The trap AI falls into Generated requirements sets are almost always functional-heavy. Fraud detection, auto-routing, claimant notifications — all functional, all satisfying, all easy to demo. NFRs are the boring ones that don't demo well, so they get thin or skipped: no latency target, no encryption standard, no retention period. An architect's first pass over any AI-drafted BRD should be counting NFRs before reading a single FR.

2. User Stories & Acceptance Criteria

A user story is a requirement written from the perspective of the person or system that benefits from it, in the form: As a <role>, I want <capability>, so that <benefit>. The "so that" clause is not filler — it's the only part of the story that lets a reviewer tell whether the capability is worth building at all. A story with no benefit clause, or a benefit clause that just restates the capability, is a red flag.

Acceptance criteria (AC) turn a story into something testable, most reliably in Given / When / Then form: Given a starting state, When an action happens, Then an observable, checkable outcome occurs. If you cannot picture a tester ticking a pass/fail box against an AC, it isn't one yet — it's a restatement of the story.

ElementWeak versionStrong version
StoryAs a user, I want fraud detection.As the fraud detection service, I want to score every claim for fraud indicators at intake, so that suspicious claims reach SIU before payout rather than after.
ACThe system should flag fraud accurately.Given a claim fraud score ≥ 75, When scoring completes, Then an SIU case is created with an evidence summary within the same processing cycle.

3. MoSCoW Prioritization

MoSCoW forces an honest conversation about what "in scope" actually means for a given release: Must have (the release fails without it), Should have (important, painful to omit, but not fatal), Could have (welcome if time allows), Won't have this time (explicitly, on the record, deferred — not forgotten, not silently dropped).

The tell of a lazy requirements set Every single item marked Must. That isn't prioritization, it's the absence of prioritization, and it means the team will discover the real priorities under deadline pressure instead of on paper. A healthy Phase 1 scope for a focused release is still Must-heavy — that's normal — but it should have visible Should, Could, and at least one honest Won't.

4. Personas

A persona is a composite of a real user type, built around goals and frustrations, not demographics. "Dana, 34, uses an iPhone" tells you nothing about what to build. "Dana needs to close accurate claims fast and is fighting five different systems that don't talk to each other" tells you exactly what the AI triage feature has to fix. Demographics are decoration; goals and frustrations are design constraints.

5. Use Cases

A use case describes a complete interaction between an actor and the system to achieve a goal — not just the happy path, but the main success scenario, the alternate flows (valid variations, like a claimant attaching photos mid-submission), and the exception flows (things going wrong: a timeout, a fraud flag, a low-confidence extraction). A use case with only a happy path is a demo script, not a use case — it tells you nothing about what the system does when reality misbehaves, which is most of the time.

6. Traceability

Every requirement should be able to answer: which business objective does this serve, which user story or use case implements it, and which test proves it works? A Requirements Traceability Matrix (RTM) makes those links explicit in one table. Its real job is surfacing orphans: a requirement nobody is testing, or a test nobody can trace back to a requirement anyone asked for. Both are waste, and both are common when requirements are generated quickly.

How these six fit together Personas motivate use cases. Use cases decompose into user stories. Stories carry acceptance criteria. Criteria are what tests are written against. MoSCoW says which of them ship first. The RTM proves the chain wasn't broken anywhere. See all nine together, worked through for one real initiative, starting at the Executive Summary.

Good vs. Bad Requirements

Four pairs that show the exact rewrite an architect should demand when an AI-generated requirement is vague enough to mean anything — and therefore nothing.

Bad (as generated)Why it failsGood (after architect review)
"The system should be fast." No number, no measurement point, no percentile. "Fast" cannot be tested or disputed — it can only be argued about after launch. NFR-010 AI extraction and triage decision must return within 8 seconds for the 95th percentile of FNOL submissions, measured at the API gateway.
"The system should handle fraud well." "Well" has no threshold, no false-positive tolerance, and no owner for the outcome. It also hides a real tradeoff (catch more fraud vs. burden more legitimate claimants) that leadership should decide on, not an algorithm by default. FR-090 / FR-100 Score every claim for fraud indicators at intake; auto-create an SIU case when the score exceeds 75, targeting a 30% improvement in early fraud-signal detection without increasing false-positive investigation load by more than 10%.
"Users should be happy with the new process." Unfalsifiable. There is no evidence that could ever prove this requirement wasn't met, which means it will always be claimed as met. KPI Maintain or improve claimant CSAT from a 4.1/5 baseline, measured via post-resolution survey, sampled monthly.
"The system should be secure." Security is not one requirement, it's a category. Left this vague, engineering picks the interpretation that's cheapest to build, not the one compliance needs. NFR-030 All claim PII encrypted at rest (AES-256) and in transit (TLS 1.2+), with audit logging retained a minimum of 7 years per state DOI requirements.
The quick test For any requirement, ask: could two competent engineers build genuinely different things and both honestly claim they met it? If yes, it isn't done yet — regardless of who or what wrote it.

KPIs & Measurable Outcomes

A requirements set with no KPIs behind it is a wish list. KPIs are what turn "this initiative succeeded" from an opinion into a measurement.

Every KPI needs four things to be usable: a baseline (where things stand today), a target (a specific number, by a specific date), a measurement method (how it's actually captured, not just asserted), and an owner (who is accountable if it's missed). The five ClaimSight AI KPIs below, and how they trace back to specific requirements, appear in full in the Vision & Business Case and Executive Summary documents.

Why this matters to an architect specifically A KPI table is the fastest way to sanity-check an entire requirements set. If a functional requirement doesn't serve any KPI on the list, ask why it exists. If a KPI has no requirement behind it, ask what's actually going to move that number.

The Architect's Review Lens

The checklist to run against any AI-generated requirements artifact before you approve it. Ten questions, applied in order.

  • Is every requirement testable — would a tester know exactly what to check for pass/fail?
  • Is priority assigned with MoSCoW, and is it honest — not everything marked Must?
  • Are non-functional requirements explicit and numeric (latency, uptime, encryption standard, retention period) rather than assumed?
  • Does every requirement trace to a business objective or KPI, and to at least one user story, use case, or test — no orphans in either direction?
  • Are personas grounded in goals and frustrations evidenced by the actual current-state process, not invented demographics?
  • Do use cases include alternate and exception flows, not only the happy path?
  • Is there an explicit human-in-the-loop or override path anywhere the AI makes a consequential decision?
  • Are compliance, audit, and data-retention requirements stated outright, not left to be inferred later?
  • Is scope (in and out) explicit enough that a new team member could tell what NOT to build?
  • Would the actual domain expert — the claims adjuster, the investigator, the claimant — recognize this as their real world?
If three or more of these fail Do not approve the artifact for downstream planning. Send it back with the specific failing items named — not "this needs work," but "NFR section has no numeric targets, and UC-02 has no exception flow." Specific feedback is itself a BA artifact.
The Initiative

ClaimSight AI at Meridian Mutual Insurance

The single worked example behind every document in this guide. Illustrative company, illustrative numbers — realistic enough to review the way you'd review a real one.

The company

Meridian Mutual Insurance is a fictional mid-size regional property & casualty insurer, licensed in 27 states, serving approximately 1.2 million policyholders across personal auto and homeowners lines. Claims are handled by roughly 340 adjusters organized into three tiers, plus a 14-person Special Investigations Unit (SIU) responsible for fraud investigation.

The problem

Today, First Notice of Loss (FNOL) intake is manual: a policyholder calls or fills out a web form, an intake specialist keys the details into the claims system, and a supervisor manually reads the description to assign a complexity tier and route it to an adjuster queue. The average claim sits 26 hours between submission and adjuster assignment. Fraud signals, when caught at all, are typically caught by an adjuster's gut instinct well into the claims lifecycle — often after a payout has already been issued. Straight-through processing (fully automated handling of simple, low-risk claims) barely exists: only 12% of eligible claims move without a manual triage step.

The initiative

ClaimSight AI introduces AI-assisted intake, triage, and fraud-signal detection into the FNOL process. Claimants submit a loss description in natural language (mobile app, web, or transcribed phone call); an AI extraction service pulls out structured claim data; a triage engine assigns a complexity tier and routes the claim automatically; a fraud-scoring model flags suspicious claims to SIU before, not after, payout. A human adjuster remains in the loop for every claim below a defined confidence threshold, and can override any AI decision with a logged reason.

Explicitly in scope for this release (Phase 1) Multi-channel FNOL intake with AI extraction · automatic triage tier assignment and routing · fraud-indicator scoring and SIU case creation · claimant status notifications · full audit trail for compliance.
Explicitly out of scope for this release Automated claim settlement or payment decisions (adjusters still decide and approve all payouts) · bulk-reassignment tooling for supervisors (deferred to Release 2) · commercial lines (auto/home personal lines only in Phase 1).

Problem Statement

Manual FNOL intake and triage costs Meridian Mutual on three fronts: speed (26-hour average time to adjuster assignment frustrates claimants at their most stressed moment), cost (adjusters spend hours on data entry and manual tier classification that adds no judgment value), and risk (fraud signals are caught late, after capital has already left the business, because no systematic scoring happens at intake).

Vision Statement

Within two years, a Meridian Mutual policyholder reporting a loss receives an acknowledgment in under a minute, a triage decision in under two hours, and — if their claim is one of the roughly half that are genuinely straightforward — substantially faster resolution overall, while claims carrying real fraud risk are surfaced to investigators before, not after, the company pays.

Strategic Alignment

Directly supports two standing corporate priorities: the multi-year Claims Modernization roadmap (of which this is the first AI-driven workstream) and the Combined Ratio Improvement target set by the CFO's office, which names loss adjustment expense (LAE) reduction as a primary lever.

C — AI-assisted intake, triage & fraud scoring (ClaimSight AI)Selected. Meets the speed target without headcount growth, adds a fraud-detection capability that does not exist today, and preserves human authority over every consequential decision.

Sequence Overview: UC-01 Main Flow

Claimant Submits FNOL → AI Triage → Adjuster Assignment

UC-01: Submit FNOL and Receive AI-Assisted Triage

ActorsClaimant (primary), AI Extraction & Triage Engine, Claims Adjuster
TriggerClaimant initiates a new claim after a loss event
PreconditionsActive policy; intake channel available
PostconditionsClaim record created, tiered (or pending review), audit entry written

Main Success Scenario

  1. Claimant opens the app or web portal and selects "Report a Claim."
  2. Claimant describes the loss in natural language (minimum 20 characters).
  3. System extracts loss type, date, location, and parties involved, with a confidence score.
  4. System issues a claim number and confirmation within 60 seconds.
  5. Triage engine assigns a tier (Fast-track / Standard / Complex-Investigative), confidence ≥ 85%.
  6. Fraud-scoring service evaluates the claim in parallel (see UC exception flow E1).
  7. Claim is routed to the matching adjuster queue.
  8. Claimant receives a status notification confirming assignment.

Alternate Flows

A1 — Low-confidence extraction: If extraction or triage confidence is below 85% at step 3 or 5, the claim is placed in the Human-Review queue with the AI's draft tier and reasoning visible, and does not auto-route to a production adjuster queue (see US-05).

A2 — Claimant attaches photos mid-flow: At any point before submission, the claimant may attach damage photos; each is auto-tagged with a damage category at ≥70% confidence or marked "unclassified" for adjuster review.

Exception Flows

E1 — Fraud signal exceeds threshold: If the fraud score is ≥75, an SIU case is auto-created with an evidence summary. The claimant's standard processing continues transparently — no indication of suspicion is shown to the claimant.

E2 — Extraction service timeout or unavailable: The claimant is automatically routed to a structured manual intake form; no data already entered is lost, and the claim is flagged for standard (non-AI) processing.

Business Rules

BR-1 Confidence threshold for auto-routing is 85%, configurable per state (FR-160). BR-2 Fraud case-creation threshold is 75, configurable per state. BR-3 Maximum two failed extraction attempts before live-agent handoff (FR-140).

Related requirements: FR-010, FR-020, FR-040, FR-050, FR-060, FR-070, FR-090, FR-100, NFR-010, NFR-050

UC-02: Claims Adjuster Reviews AI Triage and Handles Override

ActorsClaims Adjuster, AI Triage Engine
TriggerA new claim appears in the adjuster's queue
PreconditionsClaim has been auto-routed or sent to human review
PostconditionsTier confirmed or overridden; feedback logged

Main Success Scenario

  1. Adjuster opens a claim from their queue.
  2. System displays the AI's assigned tier and its top 3 contributing factors, in plain language.
  3. Adjuster reviews the reasoning and confirms the tier is correct.
  4. Adjuster proceeds with standard claim handling.

Alternate Flow

A1 — Adjuster disagrees with the AI tier: Adjuster selects "Override," is required to choose a reason code before saving, and the system logs the original AI tier, the override, and the reason for model feedback (FR-080).

Exception Flow

E1 — Adjuster identifies a miscategorized emergency or CAT-event claim: Adjuster escalates directly to a supervisor, bypassing standard override, and the claim is flagged for expedited handling.

Related requirements: FR-050, FR-080, NFR-050, NFR-100

UC-03: Claimant Checks Status via Self-Service Portal

ActorsClaimant
TriggerClaimant wants an update without calling in
PreconditionsClaimant has an active claim and portal credentials
PostconditionsClaimant views current status and next steps

Main Success Scenario

  1. Claimant logs into the self-service portal or app.
  2. Claimant selects the claim in question.
  3. System displays current status, timeline of milestones reached, and expected next step.

Alternate Flow

A1 — Claimant requests a live agent: Claimant selects "Talk to someone," and the system offers an immediate handoff or a callback within 15 minutes (FR-140).

Exception Flow

E1 — Portal unavailable: Claimant is redirected to the IVR/phone fallback with no loss of claim data, per the same 15-minute callback commitment.

Related requirements: FR-120, FR-130, FR-140
