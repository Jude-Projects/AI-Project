The Example Initiative: ClaimSight AI

Every document in this guide is written against one concrete, believable initiative so that the abstractions in Section 2 have somewhere to land. We assume requirements gathering is already complete and approved — your job starts at the architecture decision, not the requirements interview.

Business context (approved requirements — recap)

Company: Meridian Mutual Insurance, a regional Property & Casualty (P&C) carrier with 1.4M active policies across 14 states, serving auto, homeowners, and small-commercial lines.

Problem: First Notice of Loss (FNOL) intake is currently phone-and-fax first, routed manually into a 30-year-old COBOL/CICS mainframe ("ClaimsCore"). Average claim cycle time is 11.2 days; fraud leakage is estimated at 9.4% of paid losses; adjusters spend 46% of their time on manual triage rather than adjudication.

Approved initiative: ClaimSight AI — an intelligent claims intake and triage platform that lets policyholders self-report a claim via web or mobile, automatically extracts data from uploaded photos/documents, scores fraud risk, and routes the claim to the right adjuster queue — while remaining fully synchronized with the legacy ClaimsCore system of record during a multi-year strangler-fig migration.

2.1 · Why Solution Architecture Exists

Requirements tell you what the system must do. Solution architecture tells you how the pieces fit together so it keeps doing it — under load, under failure, under audit, under five years of feature growth, and under a different engineering team than the one that built it. Without an explicit architecture, a system still has one; it is just undocumented, accidental, and undefended.

What architecture is for

  • Converts business goals and constraints into structural decisions that are expensive to reverse later.
  • Makes trade-offs explicit before money and code are spent, not after a production incident.
  • Gives a team — human or AI — a shared vocabulary and shared diagrams so 40 engineers (or 40 agent sessions) build one coherent system, not 40 incompatible ones.
  • Creates a durable record of why, so the next person doesn't repeat a rejected idea or accidentally undo a deliberate trade-off.

Why this matters more with AI-generated designs

  • An AI agent can produce a plausible-looking architecture document in seconds. Plausible is not the same as correct, load-tested, or compliant.
  • AI agents optimize for the pattern that appears most often in training data (usually a generic microservices diagram), not necessarily the pattern that fits your load, team size, and regulatory posture.
  • Your job shifts from drafting architecture to directing and gate-keeping it — the 20% in this guide is precisely the checklist you apply to every AI-generated document before it ships.
The 80/20 of this discipline Full solution architecture education spans years: distributed systems theory, capacity modeling, formal methods, vendor-specific certifications. The 20% in this section is the subset that lets you catch the architecture mistakes that actually sink projects — missing NFR targets, unjustified style choices, undocumented trade-offs, and untested failure paths — without you needing to write the code yourself.

2.2 · Quality Attributes & Non-Functional Requirements

Functional requirements describe features. Quality attributes (also called non-functional requirements, or NFRs) describe how well the system must perform those features — and they are what actually determine the architecture. Two systems with identical features can require completely different architectures if one needs 99.99% availability and the other needs 99%.

AttributeQuestion it answersTypical measure
PerformanceHow fast, under what load?Latency (p50/p95/p99), throughput (req/s)
ScalabilityWhat happens at 3× or 10× volume?Max sustained load, time-to-scale-out
AvailabilityHow much downtime is tolerable?Uptime % (99.9 = 8.7h/yr down)
ReliabilityDoes it produce correct results consistently?Error rate, MTBF
Durability / RecoverabilityHow much data can we lose, how fast can we recover?RPO (data loss window), RTO (recovery time)
SecurityWhat is protected, from whom?Encryption standards, auth model, audit coverage
MaintainabilityHow cheaply can it be changed later?Deploy frequency, change failure rate, MTTR
ObservabilityCan we see what it's doing in production?% of calls traced, log coverage
ComplianceWhat regulations bind it?SOC 2, HIPAA, NAIC, WCAG, state DOI rules
CostWhat can we afford per unit of work?Infra cost per transaction
The single highest-leverage review question "Does every quality attribute have a number, not an adjective?" — "fast," "scalable," and "secure" are not requirements; they cannot be tested, and an AI agent cannot design against them. "p95 < 2s at 500 concurrent users" can be designed against, tested, and defended. See the full NFR Specification for ClaimSight AI as a worked example.

2.3 · Architecture Styles: Monolith vs. Services vs. Event-Driven

An architecture style is the top-level shape of the system — how components are separated, how they communicate, and what fails independently of what. There is no universally "best" style; there is only the style that fits the load, team topology, and change cadence you actually have.

StyleBest fitStrengthCost
Monolith (single deployable)Small teams, early-stage products, low operational maturitySimple to build, test, deploy, and reason about; no network calls between modulesScales as one unit; one bug can take down the whole app; slows down as the team grows past ~8-10 engineers
Modular monolithMid-size teams that want service-like boundaries without operational overheadEnforced internal boundaries, single deploy, easier extraction laterDiscipline-dependent — boundaries erode without enforcement tooling
Microservices / service-orientedMultiple independent teams, components with very different scaling needsIndependent deploy & scale per service; failure isolation; polyglot where justifiedDistributed-systems tax: network latency, partial failure, eventual consistency, much higher operational complexity
Event-driven (pub/sub, event bus)Asynchronous workflows, multiple independent consumers of the same fact, bursty loadDecouples producers from consumers; natural burst absorption; replayable audit trailHarder to trace a request end-to-end; requires idempotency and schema discipline; eventual consistency by default
How ClaimSight AI uses this ClaimSight AI is architected as event-driven microservices — justified in the SAD and formally decided in ADR-001 — specifically because FNOL intake is bursty (3× during CAT events), has multiple independent consumers of the same claim-submitted fact (fraud scoring, document ingestion, legacy sync, analytics), and must keep working even if any single downstream consumer (e.g. the OCR vendor) is degraded.

2.4 · The C4 Model

The C4 model (Context, Container, Component, Code) gives every audience the right altitude of diagram instead of one overloaded box-and-line chart that satisfies no one.

LevelShowsAudience
1. ContextYour system as one box, plus the people and external systems it talks toExecutives, business stakeholders, auditors
2. ContainerThe deployable units inside the system (services, apps, data stores, queues) and how they talkArchitects, tech leads, ops
3. ComponentThe internal building blocks of one containerEngineers working inside that container
4. CodeClass/module diagrams — usually generated on demand, rarely hand-maintainedEngineers, IDEs

This guide includes fully worked Context, Container, and Component diagrams for ClaimSight AI in Deliverable 02. Code-level diagrams are intentionally out of scope for an architecture package — they belong in the codebase, not the design document, and go stale within days if hand-maintained separately.

2.5 · Architecture Decision Records (ADRs)

An ADR is a short, immutable record of one significant architectural decision: the context that forced it, the option chosen, the options rejected and why, and the consequences accepted. ADRs are the single most effective defense against "why did we do it this way?" turning into an unanswerable question two years later.

Minimum viable ADR structure

  1. Status — Proposed / Accepted / Superseded / Deprecated
  2. Context — the forces at play: load, constraints, team, deadline, compliance
  3. Decision — the option chosen, stated as a firm sentence, not a hedge
  4. Rejected options — each alternative considered and the specific reason it lost
  5. Consequences — both the benefits gained and the costs accepted
Reviewer's tell If an AI-generated ADR has no rejected options, it is not a decision record — it is a justification written after the fact for a choice that was never actually compared against alternatives. Reject it and ask for the comparison. See Deliverable 03 for six fully worked ADRs, each with rejected options and accepted costs.

2.6 · Trade-off Analysis (ATAM-lite)

The Architecture Trade-off Analysis Method (ATAM) is the formal version; most teams need only its core idea: every architectural decision trades one quality attribute against another, and that trade must be named, not hidden. A design that only lists benefits has not been analyzed — it has been advocated for.

The ATAM-lite loop

  1. List the quality attribute scenarios that matter most (from Section 2.2).
  2. For each candidate architecture, score it against each scenario.
  3. Identify sensitivity points — decisions that strongly affect one attribute.
  4. Identify trade-off points — decisions that help one attribute while hurting another.
  5. Make the trade explicit in an ADR; do not let it stay implicit.

Worked example from ClaimSight AI

Choosing event sourcing for the Claims Write Store (ADR-004) improves auditability and recoverability (every state change is a replayable fact — valuable for DOI audits) but costs query simplicity and raises the skill floor for the team. That is a real trade-off, stated plainly, not a free win.

See Deliverable 05 for a fully scored, weighted trade-off matrix comparing cloud providers for ClaimSight AI, with costs stated alongside wins.

2.7 · Key Patterns Worth Recognizing

PatternSolvesWatch for
API Gateway / BFFOne front door for clients; hides internal service topology; tailors responses per client typeCan become a bottleneck or a dumping ground for business logic if not disciplined
CQRS (Command Query Responsibility Segregation)Read and write workloads have very different shapes/scaleAdds eventual-consistency lag between write and read models — must be an accepted trade, not a surprise
Event SourcingFull audit trail, ability to replay/rebuild stateQuery complexity, snapshotting strategy required at scale
SagaMulti-step business transaction spanning services without a distributed lockRequires explicit compensating actions for every step that can fail
Circuit BreakerStops hammering a failing dependency; fails fast instead of hangingNeeds a defined half-open retry policy, or it never recovers automatically
Strangler FigIncrementally replace a legacy system without a big-bang rewriteRequires a routing layer and long-lived dual-write/dual-read discipline
Anti-Corruption LayerIsolates your clean domain model from a legacy or vendor system's modelExtra translation code to own and test — worth it only when the legacy model is genuinely toxic

2.8 · Fitness Functions

A fitness function is an automated, repeatable check that a specific architectural characteristic still holds — turning an architecture principle from a slide into a CI gate. If "services must not call each other's databases directly" only lives in a document, it will be violated within a quarter. If it lives in a static-analysis rule that fails the build, it holds.

Characteristic to protectExample fitness function
Service boundary integrityStatic dependency-graph check: no service imports another service's internal package
Performance budgetLoad test in CI fails the build if p95 latency regresses >10%
Security postureDependency scan fails the build on any new critical CVE
Contract stabilityConsumer-driven contract test fails if a producer breaks a published schema
Reviewer's questionAsk: "Which of these principles are enforced by a fitness function, and which are enforced only by hope?" An AI-generated architecture that lists principles with no enforcement mechanism has described intent, not a durable architecture.

2.9 · Build vs. Buy

Every capability decision is really three options: build in-house, buy/license a vendor product, or a hybrid (buy the commodity part, build the differentiated part). The wrong default in either direction is expensive — building commodity infrastructure wastes engineering time; buying your core differentiator hands your competitive edge to a vendor's roadmap.

Lean toward BUY when…Lean toward BUILD when…
The capability is commodity (email delivery, payment rails, identity data enrichment)The capability is your core differentiator or IP
Time-to-market matters more than customizationYou need deep integration with proprietary data/workflow
Compliance certifications already exist in the vendor productVendor lock-in risk or per-unit vendor cost doesn't scale with your volume
Maintaining it in-house would starve your differentiated roadmapNo vendor meets your latency, data-residency, or explainability requirements

ClaimSight AI's fraud-scoring capability is a deliberate hybrid: buy third-party identity/claims-history enrichment data (LexisNexis), build the scoring model itself in-house. Full reasoning in ADR-003.

2.10 · Good vs. Bad Architecture

Signals of good architecture

  • Every quality attribute has a measurable target, tied to an actual business driver
  • The style is justified against real load numbers, not copied from a tutorial
  • Trade-offs are named, including the costs accepted
  • Failure paths are designed before the happy path ships
  • A junior engineer (or new AI agent session) can onboard from the docs alone
  • Change in one area doesn't require touching unrelated areas

Signals of bad architecture (over- and under-engineering)

  • Microservices for a system with one team and 200 daily users (over-engineering)
  • A monolith with no module boundaries at 50 engineers and rising incident rate (under-engineering)
  • NFRs stated as adjectives ("fast," "secure") with no number attached
  • Diagrams that don't match what's actually deployed
  • Every decision framed as having no downside — a tell that trade-offs were never actually analyzed
  • Security or compliance treated as a phase-2 add-on instead of a boundary condition from day one

2.12 · The Architect's Review Lens

This is the checklist you actually run, every time, against an AI-generated architecture document before you approve it. It is the single most reusable artifact in this guide.

  • Numbers, not adjectives. Does every NFR have a measurable target and a measurement method?
  • Style justified by load. Is the architecture style justified against actual (or credibly estimated) volume, concurrency, and team size — not chosen by default?
  • Rejected options present. Does every significant decision show what was considered and rejected, with reasons?
  • Costs stated, not just wins. Does every trade-off name what was given up, not only what was gained?
  • Failure path designed. For every external dependency: timeout, retry policy, fallback, and dead-letter/escalation path?
  • Idempotency covered. Can every side-effecting operation safely run twice?
  • Security boundary explicit. Is trust-boundary input validated, are secrets externalized, is auth checked on every protected route?
  • Diagrams match reality. Do the C4 diagrams name the actual containers/services being built, not placeholders?
  • Fitness functions named. Which principles are enforced by an automated check vs. only documented?
  • Right-sized, not gold-plated. Is the complexity proportional to the actual scale and team, or has the design over-built for a hypothetical future?
  • Sign-off traceable. Is there a clear Prepared / Reviewed / Approved trail so accountability is never ambiguous?

1 · Introduction & Purpose

This Solution Architecture Document (SAD) is the authoritative technical design for ClaimSight AI, Meridian Mutual's initiative to modernize First Notice of Loss (FNOL) intake and claims triage. It translates the approved business requirements (see the initiative recap in the field guide's Initiative section) into a system structure, and is the baseline against which C4 diagrams, ADRs, the NFR specification, and the risk register are all cross-referenced. 3 · Scope & Assumptions

In scope

  • Web and mobile FNOL self-service intake
  • Document/photo ingestion and OCR extraction
  • Automated fraud risk scoring and explainability logging
  • Triage & routing to the correct adjuster queue
  • Bi-directional synchronization with ClaimsCore (legacy)
  • Adjuster and claims-ops facing dashboards

Out of scope (this phase)

  • Claims payment/disbursement processing logic (remains in ClaimsCore; ClaimSight AI only triggers it)
  • Underwriting and policy issuance systems
  • Full legacy mainframe decommission (multi-year follow-on initiative)
  • Commercial-lines large-loss/catastrophe claims above $250K (routed to specialist desk unchanged)

Key assumptions (each logged per the accelerator's implementation-decision autonomy model): AWS is the approved cloud provider (see trade-off matrix); ClaimsCore exposes an MQ-based integration point in addition to nightly batch; Meridian Mutual's existing SOC 2 Type II program extends to cover this platform; a third-party OCR vendor is preferred over building OCR in-house.

4 · Architecture Style & Justification

Chosen style: event-driven microservices, fronted by an API Gateway / BFF, with a Kafka-based event backbone as the system's spine. Full comparison against a modular-monolith alternative is recorded in ADR-001; this section summarizes the justification against the drivers in §2.

DriverHow the chosen style satisfies it
D1 (burst)Kafka absorbs burst writes; consumers scale independently and catch up asynchronously rather than rejecting load
D2 (fan-out)One claim.submitted event is consumed independently by fraud scoring, document ingestion, legacy sync, and analytics — no consumer blocks another
D3 (legacy coexistence)The Legacy Integration Adapter acts as an anti-corruption layer and strangler-fig seam, isolating ClaimsCore's data model from the new services
D4 (audit trail)Event sourcing on the Claims Write Store gives a replayable, immutable history of every claim state transition and fraud decision
Cost acceptedEvent-driven microservices trade implementation and operational simplicity for scalability and independent failure isolation. Meridian Mutual accepts a higher operational floor (Kafka operations, distributed tracing, schema governance) in exchange for burst resilience and legacy decoupling. This cost is explicit, not hidden.

6 · Data Architecture

Core entities: Claim, Claimant, Document, FraudScore, Adjuster, Assignment, Payment (reference only — owned by ClaimsCore). See the ERD in Deliverable 06 for the full entity-relationship model.

  • System of record: ClaimsCore remains authoritative for policy and payment data during the migration; ClaimSight AI is authoritative for intake, triage, and fraud-scoring data.
  • Write path: event-sourced — every state transition is appended as an immutable event; current state is a projection.
  • Read path: CQRS-denormalized views in the Claims Read Store, rebuilt from the event stream; OpenSearch indexes support adjuster full-text search.
  • Retention: claim events retained 10 years (state DOI record-retention requirement); uploaded documents retained per Meridian Mutual's records-retention schedule with encrypted cold storage after 18 months.
  • PII handling: claimant PII is field-level encrypted at rest; photo uploads are stripped of GPS/EXIF metadata on ingestion (see Risk R-05).

8 · Security Architecture

  • AuthN/AuthZ: OIDC-based identity for policyholders (web/mobile); SSO + MFA + RBAC for internal adjuster/ops users. Every protected route validates session, role, and resource ownership.
  • Encryption: TLS 1.2+ in transit everywhere; AES-256 at rest for all data stores and object storage; field-level encryption for claimant PII.
  • Secrets: No secrets in source or images; managed secrets store injected at runtime; all secret references redacted in logs.
  • Input validation: All inbound HTTP and webhook payloads validated against a schema at the API Gateway and again at the service boundary; malformed input rejected with 400 before reaching business logic.
  • Compliance mapping: SOC 2 Type II (existing program extended), NAIC Model Bulletin on the Use of AI Systems by Insurers (explainability + bias testing on fraud model), state DOI unfair-claims-practices timelines, WCAG 2.1 AA on the policyholder-facing portal.

9 · Deployment & Infrastructure

AWS, two regions (us-east-1 primary, us-west-2 warm standby), three Availability Zones per region. Services run as containers on managed Kubernetes (EKS); Kafka on managed MSK; data stores on Aurora Multi-AZ. Autoscaling policy: services scale on queue depth and CPU, targeting time-to-scale-out < 5 minutes to absorb a CAT-event burst (see NFR-SC-01). Blue/green deployment via the CI/CD pipeline; infrastructure defined as code, no manual console changes in production.

11 · Cross-Cutting Concerns

Observability

Structured JSON logs to stdout; correlation ID generated at intake and propagated through every service call, Kafka message header, and legacy adapter call. Per-service dashboards track success rate, failure rate, retry count, and p50/p95/p99 latency.

Idempotency

Every side-effecting operation is keyed: FNOL submission by client-generated idempotency key; legacy sync by (claim_id, event_type) unique constraint with INSERT ... ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING; notification sends deduped by (recipient, claim_id, notification_type).

Failure handling

Every external call has an explicit timeout, a capped retry with backoff, and a defined fallback or fail-fast path. Failed events that exhaust retries land in a dead-letter topic with full context for manual triage — never silently dropped.

Explainability logging

Every fraud score persists the feature values, rule hits, and model version that produced it, satisfying NAIC AI Model Bulletin explainability expectations and supporting DOI audit requests.

ADR-001 · Adopt Event-Driven Microservices Over a Monolithic Extension

Accepted

Context

ClaimsCore (the legacy mainframe) cannot absorb FNOL intake load spikes of 3× baseline during CAT events, and any change to it requires a slow, high-risk mainframe release cycle. ClaimSight AI must independently scale document ingestion, OCR, and fraud scoring, each of which has a materially different load profile and failure mode.

Decision

Build ClaimSight AI as event-driven microservices publishing to a shared Kafka backbone, decoupled from ClaimsCore via an anti-corruption layer (see ADR-006), rather than extending ClaimsCore or building a single new monolithic service.

Rejected options

  • Extend ClaimsCore directly. Rejected: COBOL/CICS release cycles run quarterly; cannot meet the <4-hour cycle-time target or absorb CAT-event bursts.
  • New modular monolith. Rejected: OCR and fraud scoring have fundamentally different scaling curves (I/O-bound vendor calls vs. GPU-backed inference) than intake; a single deployable would force all components to scale together, wasting cost during normal-volume periods.

Consequences

Gained: independent scaling per component; burst absorption via the event bus; failure isolation (an OCR outage doesn't block intake). Accepted cost: distributed-systems operational tax — the team must own Kafka operations, distributed tracing, and eventual-consistency handling that a monolith would not require.

ADR-002 · Use Managed Kafka (Amazon MSK) as the Claims Event Backbone

Accepted

Context

The event backbone must support multiple independent consumer groups reading the same event (fraud scoring, document ingestion, legacy sync, analytics), ordered delivery per claim, and replay for audit/reconciliation after an incident.

Decision

Use Amazon MSK (managed Apache Kafka) as the Claims Event Bus.

Rejected options

  • Amazon SQS + SNS fan-out. Rejected: SQS lacks native replay and long retention; reconstructing claim history for a DOI audit would require custom archival tooling Kafka provides natively.
  • RabbitMQ (self-managed or managed). Rejected: weaker native support for long-retention replay and multiple independent consumer groups at this event volume; team has deeper existing Kafka operational experience.

Consequences

Gained: per-partition ordering, long retention for replay/audit, mature consumer-group model for independent scaling. Accepted cost: MSK cluster operational overhead and partition/schema governance discipline that SQS would not have required.

ADR-003 · Hybrid Build/Buy for Fraud Scoring

Accepted

Context

Fraud scoring needs both broad identity/claims-history signal (expensive to build in-house) and tight integration with Meridian Mutual's proprietary claim and policy data plus full explainability for NAIC/DOI audit — something a black-box vendor product cannot guarantee.

Decision

Buy third-party identity and claims-history enrichment data from a bureau vendor (LexisNexis-class); build the scoring model, rules engine, and explainability logging in-house on SageMaker.

Rejected options

  • Full buy (e.g. a turnkey fraud-detection SaaS). Rejected: vendor scoring is a black box that cannot satisfy the NAIC explainability requirement, and per-claim vendor pricing does not scale economically at Meridian Mutual's volume.
  • Full build (including identity/history data). Rejected: replicating bureau-grade identity and claims-history data is multi-year, high-cost work with no differentiation value — pure commodity data.

Consequences

Gained: explainable, auditable, in-house-owned scoring logic; commodity data sourced cheaply. Accepted cost: Meridian Mutual owns model drift monitoring, bias testing, and retraining — responsibilities a full-buy option would have shifted to the vendor.

ADR-004 · Event-Sourced Aurora PostgreSQL for the Claims Write Store

Accepted

Context

The claims write store must produce a replayable, immutable audit trail for DOI/NAIC review, support strong consistency for claim-state transitions, and integrate cleanly with the team's existing relational expertise.

Decision

Use Aurora PostgreSQL with an event-sourced schema (append-only event table, projected current-state views) as the Claims Write Store.

Rejected options

  • DynamoDB (NoSQL, state-only). Rejected: would require bolting on a separate audit-log mechanism to reconstruct "what happened when," duplicating what event sourcing gives natively; team has limited DynamoDB single-table-design experience.
  • Aurora PostgreSQL, state-only (no event sourcing). Rejected: satisfies query simplicity but does not natively provide the replayable audit trail the NAIC/DOI driver requires without a bolted-on change-data-capture layer.

Consequences

Gained: native replayable audit trail; strong consistency; team can reuse existing PostgreSQL operational skill. Accepted cost: query complexity rises (must project state from events) and the team must implement a snapshotting strategy as event volume grows.

ADR-005 · API Gateway + BFF Pattern Over Direct Client-to-Service Calls

Accepted

Context

Three distinct client types (policyholder web, policyholder mobile, internal adjuster dashboard) need different response shapes, and none should need to know the internal service topology or be able to call internal services directly.

Decision

Front all client traffic through a managed API Gateway acting as a Backend-for-Frontend (BFF), which is the only component permitted to call internal services directly.

Rejected options

  • Direct client-to-service calls. Rejected: exposes internal service topology to the public internet, multiplies the auth-enforcement surface, and couples every client release to every service's API shape.

Consequences

Gained: single enforcement point for auth, rate limiting, and input validation; internal services can change without breaking clients. Accepted cost: the gateway is a shared dependency and potential bottleneck — it must be load-tested and scaled independently of any one backend service.

ADR-006 · Strangler Fig Migration for the Legacy ClaimsCore Mainframe

Accepted

Context

ClaimsCore remains the system of record for policy and payment data and cannot be replaced in one release without unacceptable business risk. ClaimSight AI must coexist with it for a multi-year migration window.

Decision

Introduce a Legacy Integration Adapter as an anti-corruption layer and strangler-fig seam: new capability is built in ClaimSight AI first, with the adapter keeping ClaimsCore synchronized via real-time MQ plus nightly batch reconciliation, until each capability can be safely retired from the mainframe.

Rejected options

  • Big-bang rewrite/cutover. Rejected: unacceptable business risk for a system of record handling active policyholder payments; no rollback path if a defect is found post-cutover.
  • Permanent dual-write from every service directly into ClaimsCore's data model. Rejected: would leak ClaimsCore's legacy data model into every new service, defeating the purpose of the migration and creating N points of coupling instead of one.

Consequences

Gained: incremental, reversible migration; ClaimsCore's legacy model is isolated behind one seam. Accepted cost: the adapter is a long-lived piece of infrastructure (multi-year), and nightly batch reconciliation introduces a bounded window of potential drift that must be monitored (see Risk R-09).

Executive Summary

ClaimSight AI replaces phone-and-fax FNOL intake with a self-service, AI-assisted claims platform built as event-driven microservices on AWS, coexisting with the legacy ClaimsCore mainframe via a strangler-fig integration seam through the multi-year migration. The design is projected to cut FNOL-to-adjuster-assignment time from 11.2 days to under 4 hours, reduce fraud leakage from 9.4% toward 6.5% of paid losses, and absorb 3× volume bursts during catastrophe events without service degradation.
