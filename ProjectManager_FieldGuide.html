2.1 · Why Project Management Exists

Left alone, good engineers build the wrong thing on time, or the right thing too late, or the right thing on time and over budget — because scope, schedule, cost, and risk are each somebody's job, and without one person accountable for the whole, nobody trades them off on purpose. Project management is the discipline of making those trade-offs explicit and owned instead of accidental.

Without a PM function

Scope creeps quietly, risks surface as surprises, status is whatever the loudest team says, and "done" is negotiated after the fact.

With a weak PM function

A schedule exists but nobody protects the critical path; a RAID log exists but risks have no owner; status reports restate activity instead of health.

With a strong PM function

Trade-offs are visible before they become crises, every commitment has exactly one owner, and the sponsor can make a go/no-go call with real information.

Your job as architect, not PMYou will not run the day-to-day schedule. You will be handed AI-generated charters, WBSs, timelines, and status reports and asked: is this plan sound enough to bet a program on? This guide teaches the 20% that lets you answer that in minutes, not days.

2.2 · The Iron Triangle: Scope, Schedule, Cost

Scope, schedule, and cost are structurally linked through a fixed relationship: quality being the silent fourth variable that erodes when the other three are forced to stay fixed under pressure. Push one corner and at least one other corner has to move — or quality absorbs the difference invisibly, which is the outcome a good plan is designed to prevent.

Figure — The iron triangle. A sound charter names which corner is allowed to flex.
The rule an AI plan must followA charter that claims scope, schedule, and cost are all fixed with no stated flex is not disciplined — it is dishonest, and quality will pay the difference. Approve a charter only if it names which one constraint is allowed to move.

2.3 · Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)

A Work Breakdown Structure decomposes total project scope into work packages small enough to estimate, assign, and track — and, critically, the packages must sum to exactly 100% of scope at every level. A WBS is not a task list; it is a hierarchical accounting of scope, the same way a chart of accounts is a hierarchical accounting of money. If the percentages at any level don't sum to 100%, either scope is missing (a silent gap that surfaces late) or scope is double-counted (inflating the budget).

  • Every Level-1 work package has a named owner — no orphaned scope
  • Level-2 packages under a parent sum back to exactly the parent's percentage
  • Each work package has a stated deliverable or exit criterion, not just a label
  • Work packages are sized to roughly 1–4 weeks of effort — small enough to estimate honestly, large enough not to drown in bookkeeping
Reviewer's checkAsk an AI-generated WBS to show its percentages summing to 100% at both levels before you accept it — see Deliverable 02 for a worked example that does this explicitly.

2.4 · Critical Path & Float

The critical path is the longest chain of dependent work packages from start to finish — it determines the earliest possible completion date, and any slip anywhere on it slips the whole program day for day. Work packages not on the critical path have float (or slack): room to slip without moving the end date, up to the size of their float buffer.

Figure — A→B→D is longer than A→C→D, so it is critical; the C path has float equal to the time difference.
The most common AI-plan defectA generated timeline that never names its critical path, or draws every bar the same color, has not actually been analyzed — it's a calendar with boxes on it. Reject it until the critical path is named explicitly and protected with buffer.

2.5 · Milestones & Gates

A milestone marks a point in time with zero duration — a fact becomes true (a document is approved, an environment is live). A gate is a milestone with teeth: the next phase cannot start until named exit criteria are met and a named approver signs off. Gates are where governance actually happens; without them, phases blur together and nobody can point to when scope was locked.

Weak milestoneReal gate
"Architecture phase complete — 2026-08-05""Architecture Gate: Solution Architecture Document approved in writing by the Steering Committee, all NFRs have a measurement method, no High-severity architecture risk is unowned — target 2026-08-05"
"Testing done""SIT Exit: 100% of P0/P1 defects closed, zero open Sev-1, performance NFRs verified under load — sign-off by QA Lead"

2.6 · RAID: Risks, Assumptions, Issues, Dependencies

A RAID log is where a plan admits what it doesn't control. The four categories are easy to blur but mean different things:

Risk

Something that might happen and would hurt the program if it did. Needs likelihood, impact, and a mitigation.

Assumption

Something taken as true without proof, that the plan depends on. If it turns out false, re-plan.

Issue

A risk that already happened — it's real now, not hypothetical, and needs resolution, not mitigation.

Dependency

Something the program needs from outside its own control — another team, a vendor, a regulator — by a specific date.

Reviewer's checkEvery single row must have exactly one owner and a date — raised date at minimum, target/due date wherever it applies. A RAID row with no owner is not tracked, it's decoration. See Deliverable 04 for a worked example.

2.7 · RACI: One Accountable Per Activity

RACI assigns four roles per activity: Responsible (does the work, can be shared), Accountable (answers for the outcome — exactly one person, always), Consulted (gives input beforehand, two-way), Informed (told afterward, one-way). The single rule that matters most: if a row has zero or two-or-more Accountable owners, the activity has no real owner, no matter how many people are marked Responsible.

R — Responsible
A — Accountable (exactly one)
C — Consulted
I — Informed
Reviewer's checkScan every row of an AI-generated RACI for exactly one "A". Two Accountables means a fight is coming; zero means nobody will answer for it when it slips. See Deliverable 05.

2.8 · Agile vs. Waterfall vs. Hybrid

ModelHow it sequences workBest fitWatch for
WaterfallSequential phases (requirements → design → build → test → deploy), gated approvals between themRegulated, fixed-scope, hardware-coupled, or contractually fixed-price workLate discovery of problems — testing happens only after everything is built
Agile / ScrumShort, time-boxed increments (sprints) against a prioritized, changeable backlogSoftware with evolving requirements, fast feedback availableNeeds real product ownership and a stable team — weak without both
HybridWaterfall-style gates between major phases; Scrum sprints inside each phase's executionEnterprise programs with regulatory gates and evolving technical implementation — e.g. ClaimSight AIGate cadence and sprint cadence can drift out of sync if nobody reconciles them

ClaimSight AI runs Hybrid: the Steering Committee governs through fixed gates (Architecture, Foundation Complete, SIT, UAT, Go-Live), while the engineering squads inside each phase run two-week Scrum sprints against a backlog. See Deliverable 06 for a live sprint plan.

2.9 · Velocity & Burndown

Velocity is the average amount of work, in story points, a team actually completes per sprint — measured from history, never from a target. It is used to size how much a team should commit to next sprint, not to judge team performance or compare teams to each other (point scales aren't standardized across teams). A burndown chart plots remaining work against time within one sprint: an ideal straight-line pace versus the team's actual progress, making it visible early if a sprint is drifting.

Estimates carry bufferA sprint committed at exactly the trailing average velocity, with no reserve for the unknown item, is optimistic by construction. See Deliverable 06 for a sprint commitment that's deliberately under the team's velocity range.

2.10 · Status Reporting & RAG Health

A status report exists to let a sponsor make a decision in under two minutes. RAG (Red/Amber/Green) health against baseline — not against last week's report — is the mechanism: Green means on track to baseline, Amber means a defined variance exists with an active, funded recovery plan, Red means the baseline is broken and needs an executive decision now.

  • RAG is measured against the original baseline, not a quietly re-baselined target
  • Every Amber or Red has a stated cause, not just a color
  • "Decisions needed" is a named section, not buried in narrative prose
  • Progress is stated as % of scope complete (earned value), not % of time elapsed

2.13 · The Architect's Review Lens

This is the checklist the entire guide builds toward — use it every time an AI agent hands you a project plan to approve.

  • WBS work packages sum to exactly 100% of scope at every level
  • The critical path is named explicitly, not left implicit in a Gantt chart
  • Estimates carry a stated buffer or range, never a bare single point
  • Every RACI row has exactly one Accountable owner
  • Every RAID row has exactly one owner and at least a raised date
  • Milestones that function as gates have written, checkable exit criteria
  • The charter names which triangle constraint (scope/schedule/cost) is allowed to flex
  • Status RAG is measured against the original baseline, not re-baselined silently
  • Budget forecasts (EAC) are shown, not just spend-to-date
  • Sign-off is traceable to a named Preparer, Reviewer, and Approver with dates
If a plan fails this listSend it back with the specific gap named — "no Accountable owner on row 7," "no buffer on the SIT estimate" — the same way you'd request changes on a pull request. Approving a plan that fails this checklist transfers the risk to you.

This is why the Status Report's Decisions Needed section proposes a budget draw before a schedule slip — the charter, not the PM's preference, sets that order.
